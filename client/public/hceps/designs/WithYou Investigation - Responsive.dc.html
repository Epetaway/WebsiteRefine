01 HUMAN CONTEXT

Relationship apps are an odd design domain, because the thing they're organized around — a couple — is not a single user.

Almost every consumer product built for couples treats the relationship as the unit of design: one shared calendar, one shared photo stream, one shared feed of “what your partner did today.” That framing is convenient to build, and it is wrong about how relationships actually work. Being in a relationship does not mean giving up individual boundaries — processing a hard feeling privately, before deciding whether and how to bring it to a partner, is part of how people stay stable enough to have the relationship at all.

Software that treats “paired” as “merged” removes that option by default, and it usually does it silently. WithYou started as a question about whether a relationship app could hold both things at once: genuinely shared, and genuinely respectful of the boundary each person still has a right to.

WORKING NOTE

Pairing creates connection. It does not automatically create permission.

02 THE CENTRAL ARGUMENT

Shared software should not assume shared ownership of everything.

Everything downstream in this study follows that one sentence — and the argument arc it forces is not really optional once you take it seriously. Each step commits you to the next one.

01 — INTERACTION MODEL

Sharing has to be a first-class step, not a setting buried in a menu.

02 — DATA MODEL

Private and shared content can't be the same field with a visibility flag bolted on after the fact.

03 — PERMISSIONS LAYER

Enforcement has to live where the data is fetched, not just where it's displayed.

04 — REAL-TIME BEHAVIOR

An event that reveals shared content can't be broadcast anywhere the unshared version could leak.

05 — TRUST · OPEN QUESTION

Does a person understand, moment to moment, what their partner can and can't see? This study does not resolve it.

03 UX → ENGINEERING

One switch, six systems.

From the outside, the sharing decision is one screen: a label, a switch, a line of copy. “Share this with {{ partnerName }}.” On or off. Underneath that one switch sits everything the privacy architecture just described.

The simpler a privacy decision should feel to the user, the more carefully the underlying system may need to be designed.

HOVER EACH LAYER →
S·01 Ownership Which account authored this field — and therefore who is allowed to change its sharing state.
S·02 Pairing state Whether there's an active partner to share with at all; the toggle has no meaning for an unpaired account.
S·03 Relationship scoping The share target is a specific relationship ID, not “everyone who can see this user.”
S·04 Partner notification The partner learns a shared summary exists — without the raw text ever transiting a channel their client can read.
S·05 Cross-device consistency The same sharing state has to hold if the author checks the entry again from a different device.
S·06 Real-time scoping A live summary reaches only sockets in this specific relationship's room — scoping that is structural, not a filter after the fact.

05 REFLECTION

I began by thinking about what couples could share. I ended up realizing that the harder design problem was deciding what should not automatically be shared.

Privacy is not a feature added after the interaction model. It changes the structure of the entire product.

PRELIMINARY — Everything above describes what the system does and does not do today. None of it has been validated with couples outside my own QA accounts — the trust, comprehension, and relationship-outcome questions are open, not findings.

OPEN QUESTIONS

Do users understand the visibility model in the moment, when deciding whether to share something that matters?

When does intentional friction feel protective, and when does it just feel frustrating?

How should AI participate in emotionally sensitive contexts at all?
